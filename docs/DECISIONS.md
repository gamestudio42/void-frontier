# Decisions

Short architecture and design decision records. Newest last.

## 001. License: AGPL-3.0-or-later

The specification suggested GPL-3.0-or-later. The GameStudio42 policy for complete game code is AGPL-3.0-or-later, so the code uses it. Documentation is CC-BY-4.0; procedural art is generated by the code. Third-party assets will be listed in `docs/assets.md` with their own licenses.

## 002. English source strings, pt-BR translation

The specification set Portuguese as the initial language. Studio policy keeps English as the source language for player-facing text. The game ships both and opens in Portuguese when the browser prefers it, so Portuguese players see Portuguese first.

## 003. Own integrator, no Rapier yet

Motion is integrated by `integrationSystem` (semi-implicit Euler). Milestone 1 has no collisions, so Rapier is not installed. Milestone 2 will decide how collisions are detected; motion stays in one place.

## 004. Deterministic trigonometry in the kernel

`Math.sin`/`cos`/`atan2` are not guaranteed identical across JavaScript engines. Saves, replays and future AI testing need the same inputs to give the same state, so the kernel implements them with polynomials (error below 1e-12) and ESLint forbids the native ones in deterministic packages.

## 005. No state library

The HUD reads a tiny external store through `useSyncExternalStore`. Zustand, suggested by the specification, would add a dependency for one screen. Revisit when station menus need shared UI state.

## 006. Fuel has mass

Fuel counts toward ship mass. A full tank is about 6% of the scout's mass, so the effect is small but honest, and it makes long hauls feel lighter at the end.

## 007. Assists are deadbeat controllers on thrusters

Flight assist computes the force that would cancel a velocity component in one step and clamps it to the available thrust. That stops without oscillation and can never exceed what the thrusters deliver. Mouse steering limits the commanded turn rate by the rated maximum, a braking curve (√(2·α·0.8·error)) and a proportional term, which settles without overshoot.

## 008. Newtonian flight is the default mode

The specification makes inertia the core of the game. The game starts in Newtonian mode with the controls panel explaining the instruments; C switches to assisted flight at any time.

## 009. Drop time instead of catching up

The frame loop accepts at most 0.25 s per frame and 8 steps per frame. Anything beyond is dropped and reported in the debug overlay. The game slows down on a slow machine instead of freezing in a catch-up spiral.

## 010. Own collision system instead of Rapier

Milestone 2 needed collisions between ships, rocks and a station. Rapier would have brought a WebAssembly dependency and a second rigid-body solver beside our integrator. The shapes are all circles (ships use three along the hull, the station a few hundred along its modules), so a small system covers it: a uniform grid broad phase, circle pairs, a swept test for fast movers and an impulse solver with friction. It is deterministic, has no dependency and costs about 0.6 ms per step for 430 bodies. Revisit if we need convex polygons or stacking.

## 011. Damage from impact energy

Hull damage = (½ · reduced mass · (v² − v_safe²)) / 1000 · k, with v_safe = 3 m/s and k = 0.22 hull per kJ (`packages/content/data/physics.json`). Gentle docking bumps are free; a 10 m/s hit on a rock costs the scout about 9% hull, 26 m/s about 62%, and about 33 m/s is fatal. Energy rather than speed means a heavy hauler suffers more from the same impact, which supports the "ships are machines" pillar.

## 012. Visuals and colliders come from one layout

Station modules are data (`packages/content/data/stations.json`). The simulation turns them into collision circles; the renderer turns them into meshes. Ships therefore hit exactly what they see, and new stations need data, not code.

## 013. Adaptive graphics quality

The game starts at high quality and steps down (high → medium → low) after three seconds below 45 FPS. Low turns off shadows and bloom and renders at 0.75× resolution. `G` picks a level manually and disables the automatic choice; `?quality=low|medium|high` in the URL pins one, which the end-to-end tests use with the software renderer.

## 014. Wrecks lose their collider

A destroyed ship keeps its body, so the camera eases to a stop, but loses its collider. An invisible wreck bouncing off rocks would create sparks with no visible cause.

## 015. Collisions bypass shields

Shields stop weapons, not rocks. Impact damage still goes straight to the hull (decision 011), so careless flying stays dangerous even with full shields, and the two damage paths stay easy to reason about.

## 016. Fixed guns with a small gimbal, not turrets

Guns fire along the nose and can swivel a few degrees (6° autocannon, 3° laser) toward the selected target's lead point. Aiming stays a piloting skill, which is the core pillar, while the gimbal forgives small errors. Turrets can arrive later as a module for bigger ships.

## 017. AI pilots produce ShipCommands

Pirate AI never touches bodies. It decides a desired velocity (patrol, engage, retreat, plus obstacle avoidance) and an aim point, and converts the velocity error into thrust and strafe commands. A test checks that no AI velocity change exceeds what its engines can deliver in one step.

## 018. Combat camera framing

With a target within 650 m, the camera eases back and shifts toward the midpoint so both ships stay on screen. Without it, the preferred pirate range (230 m) put most fights off screen at the default zoom.

## 019. Ore leaves the rock as chunks the tractor collects

Mining could have added ore straight to the hold. Ejecting chunks from the cut and pulling them in with a short-range tractor keeps mining physical: you have to stay close, chunks drift if you leave, a full hold leaves ore floating, and jettisoned cargo is a real object. It also gives pirates' salvage the same path.

## 020. Hold mass is the binding limit

Ore units are dense (70–100 kg for 0.03 m³), so the Kestrel's 6 t mass limit fills long before its 20 m³ volume. A full hold drops main acceleration from 18.8 to about 11 m/s², which is the "ships are machines" pillar made concrete. Volume will matter for bulky trade goods later.

## 021. R scans, and relaunches only when destroyed

The specification puts the scanner on R. Relaunch moved to the same key because it is only meaningful when the ship is gone; the old test-cargo key (L) is retired now that real cargo exists.

## 022. Docking is a check, not an autopilot

The specification asks for docking as a skill. The station accepts a request (F) only when the ship is inside the bay, slow and aligned, and there are no hostiles nearby. There is no docking autopilot; the corridor, ring and checklist teach the approach instead. Flight assist makes it easy for beginners.

## 023. Prices from scarcity, priced unit by unit

Price = base × clamp(√(base stock / stock), 0.5, 2.5), with a 12% ask markup and a 10% bid discount. Trades are priced one unit at a time, so dumping a full hold earns less per unit than the first sale, and stock drifts back to its base level (about 3% of the gap per minute). This keeps prices sane without a full economy simulation, which the specification defers to version 0.4.

## 024. Upgrades rebuild the ship definition

Ships keep their base definition and a list of installed upgrades; the effective definition is rebuilt from both. Every system keeps reading `ship.definition`, so upgrades need no special cases. Each upgrade also adds equipment mass, so nothing is free in flight.

## 025. Insurance instead of permadeath

Losing the ship relaunches the player from the station with upgrades refitted and 90% of credits; the hold and its contents are lost. Death costs time and cargo, not hours of progress. Saves arrive in the polish milestone.

## 026. Deliveries end in flight, other contracts at the station

A delivery completes the moment the ship stops within 60 m of the relay beacon: the flight is the work, and coming home is optional. Mining and bounty contracts are handed in at the station, which keeps the "return and dock" beat of the core loop meaningful.

## 027. Contract crates are heavy and unsellable

Sealed crates weigh 120 kg a unit, so a delivery loads 1–3 t and changes how the ship flies. The station does not buy them, so a contract cannot be turned into a market trade, and jettisoning them means the delivery cannot be completed.

## 028. Reputation as one number

The specification keeps factions for later. Reputation with the outpost is a single number from -100 to +100: +0.5% payout per point, elite contracts (bigger, +60% pay) from +20. One number is enough to make failing a contract cost something without a faction system.

## 029. Saves rebuild the sector, then apply a delta

A save stores the sector id and seed plus what the player changed: the ship, credits, markets, every surviving rock's motion and ore, loose ore, contracts and the gameplay RNG state. Loading regenerates the sector from its seed and applies the snapshot. Pirates and rounds in flight are not saved; pirate groups start fresh. Saves are small (tens of kB), and a test checks that save → load → save gives the same data.

## 030. Integrity and migrations from day one

Every save file carries a format tag, a version and an FNV-1a checksum of its payload. Loading verifies the checksum, refuses files from a newer version and runs a migration chain for older ones. The chain is empty at version 1, but the slot exists so the next format change is a function, not a rewrite.

## 031. Synthesised audio

The game ships no audio files: every sound is built with Web Audio from noise and oscillators. That keeps the build small, avoids licensing third-party sound, and lets sounds follow the simulation (engine pitch tracks throttle, beams hum while they fire). Recorded or composed audio can replace individual sounds later without changing the event wiring.

## 032. Tutorial checks the simulation, not the keys

Each tutorial step watches the game state (speed, heading, flight mode, scan events, hold contents, docking, credits) instead of listening for key presses. The player can use any control scheme, and a step completes only when the thing it teaches has actually happened.

## 033. One simulation per sector, memory for the rest

Only the sector the player is in is simulated. Leaving it stores a snapshot (rocks, markets, loose ore, RNG state); entering it rebuilds the sector from its seed and applies the snapshot. Pirates start fresh on each visit. The simulation clock is carried across jumps, so contract deadlines and cooldowns stay consistent. This keeps the cost of a sector constant no matter how many exist.

## 034. Gravity as a force

The anomaly's well adds a = k / d² (capped inside a core radius) to ship forces before integration, like any thruster. Flight assist, the brake and AI all have to fight it with real thrust, and a loaded ship escapes more slowly. Rocks are not pulled; they read as being in stable orbits.

## 035. Contracts belong to stations, the board is global

Every friendly station has its own offers; active contracts, history and reputation are shared. Deliveries can target a relay in any sector or another station (completed by docking there), and mining and bounty contracts can be handed in at any friendly station. Waypoints route through the gate network with a breadth-first search.

## 036. Save format 2

Saves gained a `sectors` map and a discovery log; contracts gained their issuer and destination sector. The 1 → 2 migration moves the single sector's state under `sectors` and tags existing contracts as issued by Outpost Alpha. A test builds a version 1 save and loads it.

## 037. Subsystems from the hit position

A hit's position along the hull picks the system it damages, scaled so a quarter of the hull's worth of damage wrecks a system. The data model is five systems plus one health value per weapon mount, not per-module geometry. It makes flanking matter (engines are at the tail) without a penetration model, which can come later.

## 038. Crippled, never stranded

Wrecked engines still give 35% thrust, and a wrecked reactor or shield generator degrades the ship rather than stopping it. Losing a system costs speed and safety, but the player can still limp home and pay for repairs.

## 039. Reputation by faction, shared through the board

Reputation is a map by faction on the contract board, and each world reads the same object. Contracts move their issuer's reputation; pirate kills raise every lawful faction's. The Free Pirates sit at -100 and cannot be won over yet.

## 040. Modules occupy slots

Upgrades became modules with a slot kind. Ships have engine, utility and defence slots, so builds involve trade-offs (scanner or tractor?), and modules can be removed for half their price. Faction-gated modules (the combat computer) give reputation a concrete reward.

## 041. One economy for every sector

Station markets moved out of the sector snapshots into a global economy object passed into every simulation, like the contract board. Production, consumption, events and haulers tick in every sector on one clock, so the world keeps moving while the player is elsewhere.

## 042. Haulers are real where you are, abstract elsewhere

A hauler is a route state. In the simulated sector it is a ship flying its leg with the same thrusters and AI limits as everyone else; outside it advances by the clock and rolls a per-sector pirate risk per leg. Crossing a sector boundary switches between the two without losing progress.

## 043. Events change rates and prices, not the rules

Shortages multiply consumption (and cut stock at once), gluts multiply production, rushes multiply the price a station pays, and raids multiply off-screen risk and spawn real raiders on the busiest lane. They are data in `events.json`, so new ones need no code.

## 044. Owned ships are parked player states

A ship in the hangar is the same `PlayerState` the save uses for the flying ship, plus the station key where it is parked. Boarding parks the current ship, removes its entity and spawns the chosen one docked at the same station, so no new ship representation is needed and saves only gain a `fleet` field.

## 045. Ships are boarded where they are parked

There is no remote transfer: a parked ship can be boarded or sold only while docked at its station. Location matters, and moving a cargo ship to a market is a reason to fly it there.

## 046. Shipyards are station data

Each station lists the hulls it sells, with optional faction standing requirements, in `stations.json`; hull prices live in `ships.json`. Resale pays 60% of the hull and half of each module, matching module resale.

## 047. One route runner for haulers and the fleet

Leg planning, cruising on the clock and spawning a ship partway along a leg moved from `economy.ts` to `routes.ts`. NPC haulers and fleet ships use the same code, so a fleet ship behaves like a hauler where the player is not: real in the simulated sector, clock-driven elsewhere. The move changed no hauler behaviour (the 0.4 tests pass unchanged).

## 048. Fleet orders are deterministic routines, not planners

A crewed ship follows one of three routines (mine, trade, wingman) as a small state machine: docked, travel, work, escort. There is no AI planning and no language model: the spec asks for deterministic systems first. A trader loads unit by unit only while the next unit still sells at the destination, after commission, for 8% more than it costs, so it waits instead of trading at a loss.

## 049. Pirate risk once per sector, at a quarter of a hauler's

Fleet ships roll for pirates once per sector crossed (on the way out), scaled by `fleetRisk` (0.25) and reduced by piloting skill, plus combat skill if armed. Rolling per route segment lost most fleet ships within an hour of play. Since 0.7 haulers roll once per sector too.

## 050. Passive income stays below active play

Fleet miners work at a small share of their laser's rate, pilots keep a 25% commission and draw a wage every 5 minutes. With an average pilot a Prospector nets about 3,000 cr an hour near Outpost Alpha and 10,000 to 12,000 in the belt fields; a test keeps the belt figure under 14,500. The fleet is worth building, but flying still pays better: measured in 0.7, see `docs/BALANCE.md`.

## 051. A lost ship's pilot survives

When a fleet ship is destroyed, half its hold spills (in the simulated sector), the ship is gone and the pilot ejects and waits at the nearest station on the payroll. Losing a ship costs the hull and cargo, not the crew's experience.

## 052. The campaign is data on top of existing verbs

Six steps in `campaign.json`, each one objective (discover, kill, deliver, boss) and one reward. It uses only mechanics the game already has, advances by itself and has no failure state, so it runs alongside contracts and the fleet. Kills count for the ship being flown, so wingmen do not finish a kill objective.

## 053. The boss is a pirate like any other

The Dreadmaw is an encounter spawned when the player is in its sector during its step. Pirates are not saved, so leaving or reloading brings it back repaired. This costs no special case and keeps the fight from being whittled down across visits.

## 054. Hull prices rose instead of lasers falling

Measured hand mining paid for every hull within minutes. Raising prices leaves how mining feels untouched, and it also makes a fleet a real investment. The measurement is a scripted ceiling, not human play, so this may need another pass.

## 055. Hired pilots graze rocks and are serviced at every stop

Heavy hulls took hundreds of hull points from 15 m/s rock contacts, so a real fleet miner died in minutes while its off-screen twin never crashed. Fleet ships now take 10% of collision damage in flight and are refuelled and repaired free at each stop, paid for by the commission.

## 056. Weapons refit per mount, within its trigger group

A mount answers to one trigger (primary, secondary, mining), so it only takes weapons of that group: guns on primary, lasers and missiles on secondary, mining lasers on mining. This adds real choice without any new input, and a saved `loadout` beside the mount health carries it through jumps, saves and parking.

## 057. Missiles are projectiles that steer

A missile is a projectile with a target and a turn rate at constant speed. Countermeasures roll once, at launch, so the outcome is decided and deterministic. The AI fires its secondary with its guns, which is how the Dreadmaw uses missiles and also makes wingmen use their lasers.

## 058. New sectors change one rule each

The Graveyard halves shield recharge and Ember Reach slows weapon cooling. One rule per sector is easy to explain on arrival and on the map, and it changes which ship and fit suit the place.

## 059. One choice, late in the story

The verdict on Kess Varo comes after the Dreadmaw and leads to three different missions and an ending on each branch. A single late choice keeps both routes testable: a test plays each to the end.

## 060. The boss's difficulty is measured by scripted duels, not play

Scripted pilots fly each hull against the Dreadmaw and its escort. They are cruder than a person, so the numbers set a floor for tuning, not the final word; see `docs/BALANCE.md`.

## 061. Aimed fire trades damage for precision

With a target selected, T picks a system. Hull damage from player fire drops to 60%, and the system takes the full amount directly, so disabling a ship is a choice with a cost rather than a free upgrade.

## 062. Only pirates can be disabled

A pirate with dead engines or a dead reactor stops flying and firing until boarded or destroyed. The player's ship keeps limping, as decision 038 requires, so a lost engine never strands anyone. Boarding scuttles the ship and counts as a kill, so bounties and campaign kills work unchanged.

## 063. Giant structures are scenery

Giants sit far below the flight plane with no collider. They give scale without changing rock placement or saves, and each is built of instanced boxes to stay cheap.

## 064. Four combat hulls are authored meshes scaled to their hull length

Kestrel, Marauder, Bulwark and Dreadmaw are drawn from the studio ship family (atlas, roughness/metal, glow) instead of boxes. Each bake is rotated so the nose points +X and scaled so its length is `hull.length`. The collision circle stays the one in the ship data. The drawn turrets do not swivel: guns stay fixed, with the small gimbal from decision 016. Haulers, miners, the Jackal and the Lancer stay procedural; the family has no cargo hull, and reusing the interceptor would make the pirate the same shape as the scout. The Dreadmaw keeps the frigate's 22 m, so the picture stays inside the fight the circle already describes.

## 065. New games launch with flight assist on

The first thing a new pilot met was a ship that drifted forever. Assist is honest flight too: it fires the same thrusters and burns the same fuel (decision on assisted mode, GDD "Flight model"), so starting in it keeps pillar 1. The guided first flight now switches assist off to teach coasting, turning and the brake, and back on at the end. A setting (*New games start with flight assist on*) keeps Newtonian as the launch mode for those who want it, and the Playwright specs set it, since most of them test Newtonian flight.

## 066. The camera never shrinks the player's ship below a share of the screen

Speed and combat framing pulled the camera back until the scout was a few pixels across, so the flight model stopped reading. Automatic pull-back is now capped so the player's hull stays at least 10% of the screen height when cruising, 7% in a fight and 4.5% against a boss; the look-ahead and the shift toward a target are limited to keep the ship on screen. A target beyond the frame keeps its edge arrow. The wheel zoom is not capped.

## 067. Only near or hunting hostiles are selected without Tab

Auto-targeting picked a patrol at the edge of sensor range the moment the player undocked. A hostile is now selected on its own inside 700 m, or when its pilot is already hunting the player; anything else waits for Tab. The Outpost gate patrol moved about 300 m further from the station so the first minute is not spent watching it.

## 068. Collisions teach before they kill

At 0.22 hull per kJ above 3 m/s, a scout lost a third of its hull at 20 m/s and most of it at 30, while normal speeds run from 50 to 300 m/s: the first lesson about rocks was often the last. Two numbers in `physics.json` soften the low end without touching the energy model. Impact damage is scaled linearly from zero at the safe speed to the full rate at 15 m/s (`impactRampSpeed`), so it still never falls as speed rises. The shield takes 70% of each blow while it has charge (`impactShieldShare`), and the hull the rest; a crash with shields down hits as hard as before. The collision event lists what each ship lost, so effects and sound tell a shield bump from a hull hit.

## 069. The kill beat is presentation time

When the player destroys a ship the game holds for 60 ms and runs at 35% speed for 280 ms. Only the wall time fed to the fixed stepper changes: the simulation takes the same steps with the same inputs, so determinism and replays are untouched.

## 070. Long trips fly on a cruise autopilot

Crossing 2 to 8 km by hand meant minutes of holding W, then a guess at when to turn and brake, at speeds where any rock kills. J now hands the ship to an autopilot aimed at whatever station, gate or waypoint lies under the cursor. It stays honest: it only sets thruster throttles, like flight assist, so it burns real fuel and respects mass. It plans a burn, a coast, a flip and a braking burn on the main engine, falling back on the weaker reverse thrusters when there is no time to flip. It looks ahead at least as far as it needs to stop, slides round rocks with a small detour, and stops short (reporting it) when every detour is blocked. Its numbers live in `cruise.json`; the 320 m/s ceiling only bounds the plan, the ship has no speed cap. Any flight key takes control back, a pirate hunting the player within 1.5 km drops it, and so does 20 s without closing 50 m, so the autopilot never loiters. Stations are reached at the start of their approach lane, where the docking checklist takes over: docking stays a skill.

## 071. A gamepad plays the whole game on the standard mapping

The desktop build targets couches and handhelds, and nothing read a controller. The Gamepad API is polled once a frame through the same input controller as the keyboard, so a pad drives the same ship command and the same actions: the left stick adds to the thrust and strafe axes (a half-pushed stick is half throttle, which is honest thrust), the right stick points the nose the way it is pushed on screen, the triggers fire, and each face button calls the action of a key. Over a menu or a station panel the d-pad and left stick move focus through the visible controls in document order and A presses the focused one, so no screen needs its own pad code. Prompts follow the last device used: a string with a `pad.` twin in the locale files reads the twin, which names the button. Only the standard mapping is supported; vibration on hits, kills and death can be turned off in the settings.

## 072. Procedural audio gets a mix, layers and an adaptive score before any samples

The engine synthesised every sound straight into one bus, the score was a pad chord every 14 s, and a firefight stacked dozens of identical one-shots into a buzz. This pass keeps the no-files rule (decision on procedural audio) and fixes the structure, so recorded samples can later replace layers without touching the mix. Sounds go through effects, ambience, interface and music buses into a limiter and a soft clipper; explosions, hits on the player, jumps and story beats duck the music and ambience. Each one-shot is built in layers (attack, body, tail) with pitch jitter and round-robin variants, low-passed and quietened with distance, and sends to a generated space reverb. A voice budget (24 one-shots, by priority, merging the same kind fired within 30 ms) stops pile-ups. The score is five procedural stems on a 92 BPM grid (drone, pad, pulse, drums, brass) whose faders follow an intensity built from hostiles near, pilots hunting the player, recent fire, low hull and the boss; it rises in about a second and settles over several. The rules live in `audioMix.ts` with unit tests; an e2e renders a pile-up offline at full volume and checks that nothing clips.

## 073. The first sortie is scripted around a quiet pocket, and tips replace part of the checklist

Playtests showed new pilots meeting the contract board over their ship, then a locked pirate, the docking panel, the campaign card and the tutorial all at once, and learning the brake only after they had built up speed. A new game with the tutorial now launches the ship out of the bay itself (cinema bars, a camera pull-out, a gentle burn; any flight key takes over) and starts the lessons in open space. The simulation gets an explicit `holdHostiles` switch: holding removes the sector's encounter groups and stops raids, releasing schedules their return; it is a deterministic world flag, not a game-side filter, so the AI, the radar and the auto-lock all agree that the pocket is empty. The fight lesson calls `spawnSparring`, one pirate ahead of the player that is already hunting it and never respawns. The flag is not saved: a save made during the lessons loads with the groups coming back on their normal delay. Steps that only a checklist taught at the wrong moment now also live as contextual tips (`tips.ts`, pure and unit-tested): each has a condition on the telemetry, shows at most three times ever (kept in local storage), keeps gaps between tips, and the brake warning preempts calmer ones. Tip and launch numbers live in `packages/content/data/tutorial.json`. A test with 3–5 outside players, as the 1.0 roadmap plans, is still needed to tune them.

## 074. Fracture mining: a vein, a heat sweet spot and real fragments

Mining was holding a button over a rock while chunks flew to the tractor: profitable but with no skill in it. Each rock now has a rich vein (a direction in its own frame, derived from its look value so placing rocks draws nothing more from the RNG and every existing field keeps its layout) and a stress value the laser raises and space cools. The yield is the laser rate times a vein factor and a stress factor, so the cut rewards aiming at the vein and pulsing the laser to stay in a sweet spot, like an active reload. Past the fracture threshold the rock cracks: part of the ore is lost and the rest goes into two or three new asteroids with real mass and momentum, which the player can chase and keep cutting; pieces too small to hold together become loose ore. Volatile types explode instead, with area damage through the normal damage path. All of it runs in the deterministic simulation. Fragments are not part of the generated field, so sector snapshots store them separately (with type, size, ore, vein and heat) and the asteroid renderer lends them instances from a per-type pool. AI fleet miners pulse with hysteresis below the fracture point. Towing large fragments with the tractor, which the review proposed, is left out: fragments are mined where they drift. Numbers live in `packages/content/data/mining.json`; yields were measured and cut once (BALANCE.md).
